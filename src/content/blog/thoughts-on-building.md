# On Building Things

I've been building things for as long as I can remember. As a kid, it was Legos and contraptions. Now it's code and systems. The medium changes; something essential stays the same.

## Why Build?

The obvious answer is utility - you build things because they're useful. But that's not quite right. Most of my projects aren't useful to anyone except me. Even the ones that could be useful often never find users.

I think the real answer is understanding. Building something forces you to understand it in a way that reading or observing never can. You discover the gaps in your knowledge quickly - they're the places where the code doesn't work.

There's a passage from Richard Feynman I keep coming back to: "What I cannot create, I do not understand." This isn't just about physics. It's about any domain where the gap between knowing-about and knowing-how matters.

## Lessons from Projects

**Projects teach you that most problems are solvable, given enough time.** When you start a project, everything seems impossible. Six months later, it works. The thing you couldn't even imagine at the start is now something you can explain to others.

**Projects teach you that the hard part is rarely where you expect.** The technically difficult bits are often straightforward. The hard parts are usually about design: what to build, how to structure it, what to leave out.

**Projects teach you to tolerate incompleteness.** Nothing is ever done. Every project I've finished has a list of things I wish I'd done differently. At some point, you ship it anyway.

## The Shape of Learning

I've noticed that my understanding of a field changes shape after I've built something in it. Before, knowledge is flat - a collection of facts and concepts with roughly equal weight. After, it's contoured - some things are central, others peripheral, and you know why.

Quantum computing, for example. I'd read papers and textbooks, but it wasn't until I built a simulator that I really understood why some problems are hard and others aren't. The act of building creates structure.

## Against Completion

One trap I've fallen into: treating projects as things to be completed rather than spaces to explore. The best projects are the ones that keep surprising you. They become conversations rather than destinations.

This doesn't mean never shipping. It means holding completion loosely. The thing you ship is one snapshot of an ongoing investigation.

## What I'm Building Now

Always too many things. The current list includes improvements to QuSim, experiments with neural network architectures for speech, and a tool for converting handwritten equations to LaTeX.

Each one teaches me something different. That's the point.
